## Purpose
Assist with writing, editing, and refining academic essays in Chris's voice - balancing scholarly rigor with personal authenticity.

## When to Use
- Writing philosophy, ethics, or humanities papers
- Argumentative essays with thesis-driven structure
- Academic writing that requires MLA/APA formatting
- Editing drafts to sound less AI-generated
- Proofreading and revision rounds
- Structuring arguments with evidence and analysis

## Inputs Required
- Essay topic or prompt
- Course/context (e.g., PHIL 112, ENGL 115)
- Required length and format (MLA, APA)
- Source materials or readings to reference
- Personal stance or interest in the topic
- Previous drafts (if editing/revising)

## Outputs Produced
- Thesis statement and essay outline
- Section-by-section drafts
- Edited versions with style adjustments
- Works Cited / References page
- Final proofread essay

## Chris's Academic Writing Voice

### Characteristics (from analyzed samples)
- **Limited first-person**: Avoid "I think" or "I believe" except sparingly for thesis claims ("I will argue that...")
- **Academic but accessible**: Smart writing that non-experts can understand
- **Direct assertions**: State claims confidently without hedging
- **Evidence-driven**: Integrate quotes, then analyze their significance
- **Clear transitions**: Use explicit signposting between paragraphs

### Words/Phrases Chris Uses
- "This paper will argue that..."
- "This essay will explore..."
- "Therefore, this demonstrates..."
- "In conclusion, we see..."
- "Furthermore," / "Moreover,"
- "For example," / "For instance,"

### Words/Phrases to Avoid
- "cultivating" / "cultivation" (not natural to Chris's vocabulary)
- Overly formal jargon when simpler words work
- Excessive hedging ("perhaps," "might be," "it seems")
- ChatGPT-isms ("delve into," "it's important to note," using em dashes)

## Essay Structure Template

### Introduction (1 paragraph)
1. Hook or context-setting statement
2. Background on the topic/debate
3. Clear thesis statement (last 1-2 sentences)
4. Roadmap of what the paper will cover

**Example pattern:**
> [Topic] is [brief description of what it is]. Unlike [contrasting approach A] or [contrasting approach B], [topic] focuses on [key distinction]. This paper will argue that [thesis claim].

### Body Paragraphs (3-5 paragraphs)
Each paragraph follows:
1. **Topic sentence** - Main claim that supports thesis
2. **Context/setup** - Brief explanation
3. **Evidence** - Quote or paraphrase from source
4. **Analysis** - Explain what the evidence means
5. **Connection** - Link back to thesis

**Example structure:**
> A central premise of [topic] is that [claim]. [Author] explains that "[quote]" ([citation]). This means that [analysis]. Therefore, [connection to thesis].

### Conclusion (1 paragraph)
1. Restate thesis (different words)
2. Summarize main supporting arguments
3. Broader significance or implication
4. Closing thought (can include a relevant quote)

## Workflow

### Phase 1: Topic Selection & Planning
1. Review available topics
2. Consider personal interests and existing knowledge
3. Identify which topic allows strongest arguments
4. Choose a stance (for/against the claim)

### Phase 2: Outline & Research
1. Draft thesis statement
2. Identify 3-4 main supporting arguments
3. Find relevant quotes from assigned readings
4. Plan counterargument acknowledgment (if needed)
5. Organize into paragraph structure

### Phase 3: Section-by-Section Writing
1. Write introduction with clear thesis
2. Draft each body paragraph following structure
3. Write conclusion
4. Add Works Cited page

### Phase 4: Revision & Personalization
1. Review for ChatGPT-sounding phrases
2. Replace vocabulary that doesn't match Chris's style
3. Adjust to be ~5% more accessible (layman's terms)
4. Ensure limited first-person usage
5. Check flow and transitions

### Phase 5: Proofreading
1. Check MLA/APA formatting
2. Verify all citations are correct
3. Check grammar and punctuation
4. Final read-through for coherence

## Revision Checklist

### Style Adjustments
- [ ] Removed "cultivating" and similar words Chris wouldn't use
- [ ] Reduced overly academic jargon
- [ ] Sounds personal but not using excessive "I"/"me"
- [ ] Transitions are clear and explicit
- [ ] Evidence is analyzed, not just quoted

### Format Checks (MLA)
- [ ] Header with name, professor, course, date
- [ ] Title centered, no bold/underline
- [ ] 12pt Times New Roman, double-spaced
- [ ] 1-inch margins
- [ ] In-text citations: (Author Page#)
- [ ] Works Cited on separate page
- [ ] Entries alphabetized, hanging indent

## Constraints & Guardrails
- Never submit as own work without significant revision
- Always verify quotes against source material
- Maintain academic integrity - cite all sources
- Keep edits faithful to original meaning
- Preserve Chris's voice even when improving

## Example: Editing Request

**Input**: "Help me edit this paragraph to sound more like my writing"

**Process**:
1. Identify ChatGPT-isms ("It is crucial to note that...")
2. Replace with Chris's natural phrasing ("This shows that...")
3. Simplify vocabulary where appropriate
4. Maintain academic tone without being stuffy
5. Ensure claim + evidence + analysis structure

**Before**:
> It is crucial to note that Aristotle's concept of eudaimonia, or human flourishing, illustrates this idea exceptionally well. According to the philosopher, achieving eudaimonia necessitates the cultivation of virtues over a lifetime.

**After**:
> Aristotle's concept of eudaimonia, or human flourishing, illustrates this well. According to Aristotle, achieving eudaimonia requires developing virtues over a lifetime.

## Anti-Patterns
- Making essays too casual or conversational
- Over-relying on first-person throughout
- Using vocabulary Chris wouldn't naturally choose
- Leaving quotes without analysis ("quote and run")
- Generic conclusions that don't extend the argument
- Formatting inconsistencies in citations
